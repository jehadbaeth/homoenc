01

What Kepler's equation buys you

Given an orbit's shape and phase at some epoch — semi-major axis a, eccentricity e, mean anomaly M₀ — where is the satellite at an arbitrary later time t? Kepler's equation, M = E − e·sin(E), has an exact solution for the eccentric anomaly E, and from E the position falls out directly. No stepping through time, no re-deriving the trajectory — solve once, for any t.

The catch: solving it needs Newton-Raphson, E ← E − f(E)/f′(E), and CKKS has no ciphertext/ciphertext division. This repo's own earlier work (notes/FHE-HEIR/SGP4 FHE Prototype Findings.md) hit exactly this wall and concluded iterative Kepler solving doesn't work under CKKS without decrypting an intermediate value. This page is the test of whether that still holds.

02

A reciprocal that never divides

1/f′(E) = 1/(1−e·cos E) doesn't need a division operator if it's built as a multiply-only Newton-Raphson refinement instead: y ← y·(2−d·y), starting from y₀=1.

Why y₀=1 is already a good guess. STARLINK‑35712's eccentricity is 0.0001093 — the denominator 1−e cos E sits within about 1×10⁻⁴ of exactly 1.0. That's the entire reason this converges fast: a near-circular orbit makes the starting guess nearly exact already.
Round 1 is free. With y₀=1, d·y₀=d exactly, so round 1 simplifies to y₁=2−d — a plaintext-scalar affine map, not a ciphertext-ciphertext multiply.
2 total rounds is enough. Validated in plain floats (16_) against the exact reference (14_, real division): sub-meter position error.

This is not "division now works under CKKS." Division still doesn't exist. What changed is not needing it: the reciprocal is approximated with operations CKKS always had, and it stays cheap specifically because this orbit is near-circular. A more eccentric orbit would need more refinement rounds — still no division, just more multiplies.

03

Why this page runs two lanes

A wrong answer under encryption can come from two unrelated places: the approximation itself (polynomial sin/cos, the reciprocal) being imprecise, or CKKS-specific noise (fixed-point scale, bootstrap noise) on top of a correct approximation. Blaming the wrong one wastes time. So this page runs the identical operation sequence twice: once in plain floats (16_, zero encryption) and once for real (17_, OpenFHE) — and diffs both against an exact math.sin/cos, real-division reference (14_).

sin(E) and cos(E) themselves are fit as ordinary least-squares polynomials (degree 10) over the bounded domain this satellite's mean anomaly actually occupies across the ±1800s window — rescaled to [−1,1] first, the same numerical-conditioning fix the earlier TenSEAL prototype needed (15_fit_kepler_trig_polys.py). No iterated composition, unlike the sign function elsewhere in this project: sin/cos are smooth over a bounded domain, so one fit suffices.

Why zero, when Approach E needed 11 rounds and 3 bootstraps

Not because propagation is inherently cheaper than a distance comparison — the raw distance calculation in Approach E is trivially cheap too, a handful of levels. What's expensive there is everything after the distance: turning a real number into a confident yes/no. CKKS has no comparison operator, so Approach E fakes one by composing g(x)=(3x−x³)/2 on itself 11 times, sharpening a value toward a saturated ±1 — each round costs about 3 levels, and 11 rounds is roughly 33 levels against a 15-level per-cycle budget, forcing 3 refills.

This page's computation never has to fake a decision. It solves an equation and reconstructs a position — both are one-shot arithmetic, evaluated once, not sharpened iteratively toward a saturated answer. That's the entire difference: 24 levels spent once, versus roughly 33 levels spent per full sharpening pass, repeated because a single pass isn't confident enough to trust.

05

What this does and doesn't settle

It closes the specific wall this repo's own earlier findings described: an iterative Kepler solve runs under CKKS end to end, using only multiply-only Newton-Raphson and never a mid-loop decrypt. It also doesn't generalize on its own — this result leans on the orbit being near-circular (fast reciprocal convergence) and the window being short enough to avoid mean-anomaly wraparound (no range reduction implemented).

Most real perturbation dynamics don't have a closed form the way two-body motion does. The RK4 walkthrough is what happens when there's no equation to solve — only a trajectory to walk, one small step at a time. The benchmarks page puts both side by side with Approach E.
