01

What "propagate this, encrypted" actually asks for

Approach E's question was: given two positions, how far apart are they? This page's question is one layer earlier: given an orbit's shape and phase — semi-major axis, eccentricity, mean anomaly, all encrypted — where is the satellite at a given time? Nobody, including the host running the computation, should see the orbit to answer that.

There are two honest ways to answer it under CKKS, and they have completely different cost shapes:

closed form
Kepler's equation has an exact algebraic solution for any query time. Solve it once (a couple of Newton-Raphson rounds), get the answer.
general integration
Most real dynamics (J2, drag, third-body) have no closed form. The only option is walking forward in small time steps, re-evaluating the force at every one.

Both were actually run, on the same real orbital elements, on the same machine, at the same (demo, insecure) CKKS parameters. What follows is what each one actually cost.

02

Kepler: solve once, for any time

The eccentric anomaly E solves M = E − e·sin(E), where M is the (public) query time expressed as mean anomaly. Newton-Raphson refines a guess: E ← E − f(E)/f′(E). CKKS has no ciphertext division, so 1/f′(E) is built from a multiply-only Newton-Raphson reciprocal instead — it converges in one extra round here specifically because this satellite's eccentricity (0.0001093) keeps the denominator within about 1e-4 of 1.0.

1. Encrypt a, e. The only private orbit-shape inputs. Semi-major axis 6840.83 km, eccentricity 0.0001093.
2–3. Two Newton-Raphson rounds. sin(E)/cos(E) via a degree-10 polynomial fit (least squares, rescaled to [−1,1] first — see chapter 4 for why that rescaling matters). Converges to sub-meter accuracy against the exact math.sin/cos reference.
4. Reconstruct position. r = a(1−e cos E), cosν = (cos E−e)/(1−e cos E), sinν = √(1−e²) sin E/(1−e cos E) — no atan2, no branch.
5. Decrypt. One value, no intermediate ever left the encrypted domain.
Measured, all 5 query times across the ±1800s window: 0 bootstraps, 3.86s total (0.77s/query). Decrypted output matched the plain-float approximation pipeline to all 6 printed decimal digits — CKKS noise added nothing detectable on top of the ~0.8m approximation floor already measured with zero encryption.
03

RK4: no closed form, so walk forward instead

RK4 integrates d/dt[x,y,vx,vy] = [vx, vy, −x/r³, −y/r³] with four stage evaluations per step (k1..k4), each needing its own force evaluation — and every force evaluation needs 1/r³, which CKKS has neither the division nor the square root to compute directly.

The fix is the same shape as Kepler's reciprocal: Newton-Raphson, multiply-only, this time for inverse square root (y ← y(3−r²y²)/2), cubed afterward. Three rounds get it to about 1e-12 relative error. But this happens four times per step (once per RK4 stage), and each occurrence is a much longer multiplication chain than anything Kepler needed — roughly 15–20 levels per force evaluation, against Kepler's whole solve using about 10.

04

The bug: bootstrapping values that were too big

The first version of the RK4 script encrypted raw kilometre-scale positions directly — thousands of km, with r² intermediate values around 4.7×10⁷. It ran step 1 fine, then step 2 threw this on decrypt:

RuntimeError: Decode(): The decryption failed because the approximation error is too high. Check the parameters.

CKKS bootstrapping refreshes a ciphertext's depth budget by homomorphically re-running the decryption formula, using a sine-based approximation of modular reduction that's only accurate within a calibrated range. Nothing in that first attempt had rescaled the values into it — four bootstraps on values already in the tens of millions corrupted the chain badly enough that the final decrypt just failed outright.

The fix is standard in orbital mechanics: canonical units. Divide every length by a distance unit (7000 km here) and every time by a matching time unit, chosen so gravitational parameter μ becomes exactly 1. Positions and r² become O(1) quantities — comfortably inside CKKS's happy range — and the force evaluation gets simpler too, since there's no large −398600.4418 constant to multiply through anymore.

This is the same discipline Approach E already uses — SCALE_KM2 rescales distance² before any further processing, for exactly this reason. The RK4 script's first draft just skipped it, and paid for that directly, in a real traceback, not a hypothetical one.

What that error growth is, precisely

RK4's error at 4 steps comes from two independent sources, and they're separable because each was measured on its own: RK4's own discretization error (inherent to using only 4 large steps — see chapter 3's table), and CKKS/bootstrap noise on top of that. At step 4, the encrypted result differs from the plain-float approximation (zero encryption, same polynomial/reciprocal chain) by about 2.4 km — noise that compounds because each step's small error becomes part of the next step's starting state, the same compounding a plain 4-step RK4 integrator would show even with infinite-precision arithmetic, just made a little worse by CKKS.

Extrapolating to an actually accurate RK4 run

Chapter 3 showed 180 steps are needed to reach Kepler's sub-meter floor over this same window. Using the measured steady-state cost (steps 2–4 average: ~50.0 s and 4 bootstraps per step, not the artificially cheap step 1, which started from a freshly-encrypted, still-shallow ciphertext):

180 steps × 50.0 s/step ≈ 9,000 s ≈ 2.5 hours
180 steps × 4 bootstraps/step ≈ 720 bootstraps

extrapolated from a real 4-step measurement, not independently run end to end — and still at demo-grade, insecure parameters. Production security parameters would add the same 10–100× per-bootstrap penalty Approach E's own report measured for its comparison pipeline.

06

What this actually settles

Encrypted propagation is cheap when a closed form exists, and it is not a general property of "propagation." Kepler's equation happens to have one; most real perturbation models (J2, drag, third-body, anything SGP4-shaped) don't, and fall back to general integration — which this page shows costs roughly two orders of magnitude more, on the same hardware, for the same physical window, purely because it can't skip the step-by-step walk a closed form gets to skip.

The honest reframe from earlier in this project still holds and is now backed by a real measurement instead of an estimate: for short-window close-approach screening, a linear (Clohessy-Wiltshire) approximation of relative motion needs neither Kepler's iteration nor RK4's step count — a fixed, small-depth computation, plausibly cheaper than both of these. Whether it's accurate enough depends on the separations and window lengths actually in play, which is the next thing worth testing, not assuming.
