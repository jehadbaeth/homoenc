Applied case study · encrypted conjunction assessment

Can two encrypted orbit ephemerides tell you if they're about to collide?

A follow-on to our earlier CKKS/SGP4 feasibility study, this time on the question a real customer would actually ask: given two Orbit Ephemeris Messages, find the closest point of approach — without decrypting either trajectory. Three designs, implemented and cross-checked against a plaintext reference, on real Starlink tracking data.

Scenario: STARLINK-35712 × STARLINK-3845 Data: live CelesTrak TLEs Scheme: CKKS, same context as the SGP4 study
Abstract

What this study found, in short

Our earlier study asked whether CKKS could carry an orbit-propagation workload at all. This one asks the more commercially relevant question: can two parties compute a conjunction assessment — is object A about to pass close to object B, and how close — without either party's trajectory ever being decrypted? We built three designs on top of local Lagrange interpolation, whose key property is that its weights depend only on the (public) sample times, not the (potentially encrypted) sample values — a genuine privacy improvement over the polynomial-fit trick our SGP4 study depended on, which needed orbit shape to be public. Approach B (decrypt the full distance-vs-time curve) matches a plaintext reference to within ~2 cm at the conjunction itself and ~7 m at worst, at the ephemeris window's edges (3.2×10⁻⁶ relative to the 2,212 km separation there) — three orders of magnitude smaller than anything that would change an operational decision. Approach C (decrypt only a below-threshold flag per time, CDM-style) matches the plaintext threshold decision on all 61 tested points. Approach A (never decrypt anything but the final minimum) works for a single pairwise comparison but hits a real, reproducible CKKS depth wall the moment the reduction tree grows past one level — confirmed empirically, not assumed. We also tried a fourth design, Approach D (sum all per-point flags homomorphically and decrypt only the total): the mechanism is genuinely zero-disclosure and verified correct, but the resulting count is wrong (12 vs. the true 1) because the fitted comparator polynomial doesn't saturate across enough of the window, and fixing that by iterating it further hits the same depth wall as Approach A. That pointed at bootstrapping-capable CKKS as the one thing that could fix it, so we followed up with Approach E: a from-source build of OpenFHE (the pip wheel for this problem is mislabeled and doesn't run on this Darwin/arm64 machine) using its CKKS bootstrapping to sharpen the same sign polynomial through all 11 compositions the plaintext math actually needs, instead of the 2 TenSEAL/SEAL allows. Under non-production security parameters (explicitly disclosed — a real deployment needs a much larger ring dimension and would be substantially slower per bootstrap), it works: the decrypted sum comes to 59.0000 against an ideal 59, correctly implying a count of 1 — the same true answer Approach D could not reach. Getting there also surfaced two operational lessons: a badly scaled comparator polynomial produces silently wrong answers that look like they could be right (a more dangerous failure mode than an exception), and packing many points per ciphertext means only one slot per block reliably carries the correct value, so a naive whole-block sum silently corrupts the count unless masked down to that one slot explicitly. All five approaches, the plaintext cross-check, and the real orbital data used are in this repository.

Background

Conjunction assessment, and why it's a better test than propagation alone

A Orbit Ephemeris Message (OEM) is the standard format operators exchange to describe a satellite's trajectory: a table of timestamps, each with a position and velocity vector. When two operators' objects are predicted to pass near each other, one of them (or a third-party service) issues a Conjunction Data Message (CDM) — the time and distance of closest approach, and the probability of collision. This is one of the most routine, safety-critical computations in satellite operations today, and it is also exactly the kind of computation an operator might not want to hand over in the clear: your ephemeris reveals where your satellite is and, over a few samples, how it maneuvers.

Our earlier study picked SGP4 propagation as a workload because it forced a real iterative numerical method (Newton–Raphson for Kepler's equation) through CKKS's constraints. This study picks conjunction assessment for a different, complementary reason: it is the actual product question a customer is likely to ask, and — unlike propagation — it inherently involves two parties' data and a comparison ("which one is smaller," "is this below a threshold") rather than only arithmetic. Comparison and argmin are exactly the operations CKKS cannot express directly, so this workload has its own, different wall to work around, distinct from division.

The scenario

Real satellites, real TLEs, a real close approach

To satisfy "actual real life data" rather than a synthetic geometry picked to look good, we fetched live TLEs for the full public Starlink constellation from CelesTrak (10,726 objects at the time of the run), and used a k-d tree nearest-neighbor search over every object's propagated position at a fixed epoch to find a genuinely close, currently-real pair: STARLINK-35712 and STARLINK-3845. A dense plaintext SGP4 sweep (coarse ±12 h at 30 s resolution, refined to ±120 s at 0.5 s resolution) located the true closest point of approach at t = +0.5 s from the ephemeris window's center, with a miss distance of 5.4320 km — confirmed as a genuine local minimum by checking distances at several nearby offsets, not a boundary artifact.

Two OEM tables were built from this: 59 samples each at 60 s cadence, spanning ±1740 s around the conjunction, columns t, x, y, z, vx, vy, vz in km and km/s — the same shape a real OEM file has. These two tables, oem_a.csv and oem_b.csv, are the only input every stage below operates on.

What was built

One interpolation trick, three disclosure policies

Every approach needs the same building block: given the ~59 discrete OEM samples, estimate position at an arbitrary query time t that falls between samples. We use local Lagrange interpolation (order 7, an 8-point window around the query time) — the standard technique for OEM interpolation. Its critical property for this study: the interpolation weights are a function only of the sample times, which are public metadata, never of the sample values (positions), which are what we want to keep encrypted. That means homomorphic interpolation is just ciphertext.dot(weights) — a plaintext-weighted sum, costing zero multiplicative depth against other ciphertexts. This is a real privacy improvement over the SGP4 study's core trick, which required the *orbit shape* to be public in order to build its polynomial fit; here, nothing about the trajectory's shape needs to leave the client.

From that shared primitive, squared distance between the two interpolated positions at any query time t is dx² + dy² + dz² — three ciphertext subtractions and three ciphertext-ciphertext multiplications, one multiplicative level. The three approaches differ only in what they do with that distance_sq(t) value and how many of them get computed:

Approach B — bounded disclosure of the full distance curve

Compute distance_sq(t) homomorphically at a public grid of candidate times (here: 61 points, every 10 s across ±300 s), and decrypt every one of them. The client learns the full distance-vs-time curve and can find the minimum itself in plaintext. Cheapest to build, cheapest to run per point, but discloses the most: the complete shape of the encounter, not just whether it's close.

Approach C — thresholded flag disclosure

Same per-point computation, but instead of decrypting distance_sq(t) directly, compute (distance_sq(t) − threshold²), scale it into a fitted comparator polynomial's valid domain, and decrypt only the resulting ≈±1 flag. The client learns "was this time-point below the safety threshold," which is the actual CDM-style question ("is there a close approach, and roughly when") — never the miss distance or the trajectory shape behind it.

Approach A — full homomorphic argmin, nothing decrypted but the final minimum

The strongest disclosure policy: reduce all K candidate distance_sq(t) values to a single encrypted minimum via a log₂(K)-deep binary reduction tree of pairwise homomorphic min operations, and decrypt only that one final value. Homomorphic min(a,b) is built the standard CKKS way: (a+b)/2 − |a−b|/2, where |x| is approximated as x · sign_approx(x) using an offline-fitted polynomial (below). This is the approach that actually exercises this study's depth budget, and its real behavior — not the idealized version — is reported honestly in Correctness and Findings.

The comparator polynomial

Approaches A and C both need an approximate sign(x) under CKKS. We use the standard Cheon et al. composition g(x) = (3x − x³)/2, applied 3 times, which sharpens toward a step function on [-1, 1]. Composing 3 times gives a degree-3³=27 polynomial; instead of paying 3 sequential ciphertext operations, we fit that whole composition as a single degree-27 polynomial offline (max fit error 6.6×10⁻¹⁴ against the raw composition) and evaluate it in one polyval Horner pass. This polynomial is only valid — and only sign-preserving — for inputs actually inside [-1, 1]; anything scaled outside that range does not fail cleanly (see Findings).

Approach D — homomorphic flag-sum, and why it doesn't close the gap

The natural follow-up question after B/C/A: is there a case where the whole computation stays encrypted and only a final answer comes out, with zero intermediate disclosure? A, B, and C each fail that bar for a different reason — A can't finish the reduction at all past K=2, B decrypts the full curve by design, and C decrypts 61 individual flags by design. We tried one more design: sum all 61 per-point flag ciphertexts from C before decrypting anything, and decrypt only that one sum. Ciphertext addition costs zero multiplicative depth, so this is free relative to C's own per-point cost, and only one number ever leaves the encrypted domain — not which time was flagged, not any distance, not any per-point confidence.

The summation mechanism itself is verified correct: the homomorphic sum reproduces, to four decimal places, the manual plaintext sum of Approach C's own already-decrypted flags (37.3017 both ways). But the sum is not a usable proxy for "how many points are below threshold." With exactly 1 of 61 points genuinely below threshold, an ideal step-function flag would sum to 61 − 2 = 59; the actual sum is 37.3, because the fitted sign polynomial only reaches a confident ±1 outside roughly ±230 s of the conjunction (16 of the 61 points); the remaining 45 — including every point near the decision boundary — sit in its near-linear region (|flag| ≤ 0.37 within ±100 s, ≤ 0.015 within ±20 s; see Findings). Reading (n − sum) / 2 as a count gives 12, not 1, and we are not reporting that as a working estimator.

We then checked whether the standard fix — composing the sign polynomial against its own output, f(f(x)), which sharpens toward ±1 with each iteration — could rescue this without bootstrapping. In plaintext it works cleanly: sign was never wrong (Approach C already classifies all 61 points correctly with a single evaluation), but 10 extra compositions saturate all 61 flags to ±1 closely enough for their sum to be read as a count, including the hardest case (the true conjunction point, whose scaled input is only ~1.4×10⁻⁵ from zero). Under this study's actual CKKS parameters, only 2 extra compositions fit before the identical ValueError: scale out of bounds depth wall Approach A hits at K=4 — the same structural ceiling, reached from a different direction. This is genuine negative evidence, not a bug: TenSEAL/SEAL's CKKS implementation has no bootstrapping, so there is a hard limit on how many nonlinear stages one ciphertext can pass through, and this problem needs roughly five times that limit to saturate confidently across the full window. See 07_approach_d_flag_sum.py and 08_sign_iteration_depth_wall.py for the reproducible scripts.

Approach E — OpenFHE bootstrapping closes the gap Approach D left open

D's negative result traced to one specific cause: TenSEAL/SEAL's CKKS has no bootstrapping, so a ciphertext can only pass through so many nonlinear stages before it runs out of depth. That leaves a real question unanswered — is this a fundamental FHE limit, or an artifact of this one backend? OpenFHE supports CKKS bootstrapping and was the direct way to test it, but the pip wheel (openfhe 1.5.1.0.24.4) is mislabeled as universal while actually bundling x86_64-linux-gnu binaries — it does not run on this Darwin/arm64 machine. We built OpenFHE 1.5.1 and its Python bindings from source instead (Homebrew's libomp, Xcode Command Line Tools, pybind11) — a real source build, not a drop-in pip swap, documented in 09_approach_e_openfhe_bootstrap.py.

Security parameters, disclosed up front: this build uses HEStd_NotSet at ring dimension 213 — OpenFHE's own bootstrapping-demo parameters, chosen for iteration speed, not 128-bit security. A real deployment would need a ring dimension around 216–217, and bootstrapping at that size is plausibly one to two orders of magnitude slower per call. Every number below is a feasibility check on whether bootstrapping fixes the saturation problem at all, not a production latency benchmark.

The pipeline packs all 61 grid points into a single ciphertext (one 64-slot block per point, of which the Lagrange interpolation only needs 59), so one EvalInnerProduct per axis per OEM recovers all 61 interpolated positions in a single pass. From there it's the same scaled-distance-then-sign-poly pipeline as C and D, but composed against itself 11 times total — the same count the plaintext math in D's own writeup showed this scenario's hardest point actually needs to saturate — with EvalBootstrap inserted whenever the remaining depth budget dropped below 8. That took 3 bootstrap calls in total.

Building this surfaced a genuine bug, not just a tuning problem: the first working version returned a decrypted sum of 56.4375, not 59. We decrypted a full 64-slot block directly, both before and after the 11 compositions, to find the cause, and the block turned out not to be the uniform replica of one value that a naive reading of EvalInnerProduct as "batched inner product" would suggest: only slot 0 of each block reliably holds the value that matches this point's correctly computed distance, and this was true even before composition. The other 63 slots hold different, smaller-magnitude values, most likely leftover partial sums from the underlying rotate-and-add reduction rather than 59 independent Lagrange samples plus 5 padding zeros as the block layout might suggest. Those other slots sit close enough to zero to fall in the sign polynomial's unstable region, so most of them saturate to +1 over 11 self-compositions regardless of the block's true sign. We independently confirmed this by decrypting the t=0 block directly: slot 0 correctly held −1 both before and after composition, but slots 28–63 of that same 64-slot block had all saturated to +1 after composition. A naive whole-block average silently folds that majority into the result. The fix is an exact plaintext one-hot mask (1.0 at slot 0 of each block, 0.0 elsewhere) applied before EvalSum, so every slot except the one reliable one is eliminated by an exact zero instead of averaged in. We reproduced the pre-fix value independently by reverting to the naive sum against the same pipeline and got 56.437499658724306 — matching to nine significant figures, not a rounded-off approximation of the claim. We do not have a proven account of why only slot 0 is reliable; treat it as an observed property of this reduction, not a claim about "padding slots at an unstable fixed point."

Result: the decrypted sum of all 61 saturated flags is 58.99999988 against an ideal 59 for the true 1-of-61 ground truth — the same point (t=0) Approach C independently flags as below threshold. Reading (n − sum) / 2 now gives 1.0000, matching the true count of 1. This is what D could not deliver: a decrypted sum that is actually a trustworthy count at this problem's precision. The zero-per-point-disclosure design is the same as D's — the sum path never decrypts anything but the final number. Note the shipped script itself is not that minimal: it also decrypts all 61 per-point flags in a clearly separated diagnostic section, purely to verify saturation and cross-check against plaintext ground truth for this report. A real deployment of this pattern would skip that section and decrypt only the sum.

One limit worth stating plainly: 11 compositions was not a fixed constant, it was sized to this scenario's hardest point (a true distance of 5.432 km against a 10 km threshold). The script has no runtime check that saturation was actually reached — it reports the observed sum, not a pass/fail assertion — so a different scenario with a candidate sitting even closer to the threshold could need more compositions, and therefore more bootstraps, to produce an equally trustworthy sum. This is a working demonstration at this scenario's precision, not a general-purpose encrypted counter that adapts itself. Wall-clock for the full pipeline in our independent re-run, under the non-production parameters above: 811 ms context+keygen, 68 ms encrypt, 443 ms distance+scale, ~3.7 s across 3 bootstrap calls, ~1.57 s of polynomial composition work interleaved with them, 44 ms for the final masked sum and decrypt — about 6.6 s total. See 09_approach_e_openfhe_bootstrap.py for the reproducible script.

The honest way to state where this leaves D's negative result: the right conclusion was never "FHE can't do this." It's "TenSEAL/SEAL's CKKS can't do this, and OpenFHE's CKKS bootstrapping can, at a from-source-build and non-production-security-parameter cost we have not eliminated." Both are true, and both are stated here rather than picking the more flattering one.

Model 1 — the data-owning customer vs. the infrastructure operator

This is the primary case for an actual OKAPI:Orbits deployment: a customer submits their own encrypted orbital data, an operator (OKAPI:Orbits' own service, or whatever cloud it runs on) performs the homomorphic computation, and only the customer's key ever decrypts anything. In this model the operator's exposure is zero by construction, and it is the same zero for every approach below — B, C, A, D, and E all keep every intermediate value in ciphertext right up until a .decrypt() call that stands in for the customer's own machine, never the operator's. There is no privacy reason to hand this customer a smaller answer than the approach can support: they already own the satellite and the risk being assessed. So in Model 1, "the client learns X" in the table below should be read as a floor a real deployment should exceed, not a ceiling to protect — decrypt the most actionable result available (which specific candidate time is a violation, not just a count), because withholding detail from the data owner buys no privacy against the party actually being kept out.

Model 2 — two data-owning operators sharing one computation

The disclosure spectrum below is a real, live design decision when there is a second party in the loop: two satellite operators each contributing their own orbital data to the same conjunction check, where the result also has to reach a party — the counterparty, or a neutral screening service — that should not learn more of the other side's geometry than the question requires. Here, B vs. C vs. A vs. D/E is a genuine dial: full curve, thresholded flag, single minimum, or count-only, in decreasing order of what leaks toward whichever side receives and decrypts the answer.

None of these five requires either party's orbit shape to become public — the improvement over the SGP4 study's design holds across all of them. Among B, C, and A, the tradeoff is purely about how much of the distance signal the evaluating party gets to see, and it scales inversely with cost: the design that discloses the least (A) is also the one that runs into CKKS's hard depth limit first. D and E are a different kind of result: both achieve zero per-point disclosure, but D's aggregate isn't trustworthy at this problem's precision on TenSEAL/SEAL, while E's is — on a different backend, built from source, under security parameters that are explicitly not production-grade. Whether that tradeoff is worth it for a given deployment is a real decision, not a foregone conclusion.

Second scenario

A harder, real multi-event test

The single conjunction above is real data, but it is one event and it's not close to the decision boundary. To stress the pipeline against something more realistic, we scanned the same real TLE pair (STARLINK-35712 / STARLINK-3845) over a ±3 h window instead of just the one known CPA, using the same coarse-to-fine SGP4 search, generalized to report every local minimum rather than only the global one. That search found 7 real close-approach events, not constructed ones — including the original 5.432 km violation, four clearly safe events (20–62 km), and two genuine non-events sitting just outside the 10 km screening threshold:

Each cluster got its own ±40 s / 10 s-step local monitoring window (9 candidate points), the same window/step design as the single-event scenario applied per-event — 63 candidate points total, built from a single 293-sample OEM pair spanning all 7 clusters plus interpolation padding. Per the steering to focus on what actually works, this test concentrated on Approach C and Approach E; Approach D was run once to confirm its known saturation failure generalizes to a harder case, and is not re-analyzed further here — see Findings for the mechanism.

Approach C and Approach E both matched plaintext exactly

Every one of the 63 encrypted per-point results — from both Approach C (per-point ±1 flags, no bootstrapping) and Approach E (bootstrapped, using the identical TOTAL_COMPOSITIONS=11 constant tuned on the single-event scenario, not retuned for this one) — was diffed against 10_multi_plaintext_reference.py. Both scored 0 mismatches out of 63 points. Approach E's decrypted sum came out 61.0000 against an ideal of 61 (7 clusters × 9 points, 1 cluster flagged), giving an estimated violation count of exactly 1, matching ground truth. Approach D, run for comparison, produced a decrypted sum of 1.6318 against the same ideal of 61 — the same saturation failure already documented for the single-event scenario, now confirmed on a scenario with real signal spread across seven events instead of one.

That Approach E reproduces this exactly, using the single-event scenario's tuning unchanged, is the strongest result in this report: it means the fixed bootstrapping recipe is not overfit to one geometry.

The correct classification at the two near-boundary events carried essentially zero confidence margin. Approach C's raw (pre-threshold) flag values at the three points closest to each cluster's own minimum were: cluster 3 (5.432 km, the real violation) −4.76×10⁻⁵; cluster 4 (13.276 km, safe) +5.15×10⁻⁵; cluster 6 (11.698 km, safe) +2.49×10⁻⁵. All three are within noise-floor distance of zero — the sign, and therefore the classification, was correct in every case, but a violation a few hundred meters closer to the threshold on either side could plausibly flip it at this SCALE_KM2 operating point. This test demonstrates that these two specific real near-boundary cases classify correctly, not that the pipeline has comfortable margin near the decision boundary in general — those are different claims, and only the first one is proven here.
What Model 1 (customer vs. infrastructure) actually recommends here. Since the infrastructure operator's exposure is zero regardless of which approach runs, and the customer already owns the risk being assessed, a real deployment should give the customer Approach C's per-cluster detail (which of the 7 known times is the violation) rather than Approach E's aggregate-only count — decrypting more costs nothing extra in privacy against the infrastructure. Approach E's aggregate-only design earns its keep under Model 2, where the receiving party is not the data owner and a bare count is deliberately less than a per-cluster answer.
The 7 candidate times are a public input to this computation, not an output. multi_ground_truth.csv — and therefore every query time submitted to the encrypted pipeline — comes from a plaintext SGP4 search run before any ciphertext exists. Whoever runs Approach E already knows all 7 approach times going in; what stays hidden is which of them crosses the threshold and (under Approach C/E) the distances themselves. The correct claim is "1 of these 7 known times was a violation," not "there was 1 violation somewhere in a 3-hour window" — worth stating precisely rather than letting the abstract framing overstate it.
How to reproduce this

Running the pipeline

Everything lives under oem-conjunction/ in this repository. Run every stage with PYTHONPATH=oem-conjunction set, from the repo root (the scripts import shared modules by bare name):

data/find_cpa_and_build_oems.py — fetches nothing (TLEs are hardcoded from a prior CelesTrak fetch) and writes data/oem_a.csv, data/oem_b.csv, data/ground_truth.csv. 01_plaintext_reference.py — the plaintext cross-match algorithm. 02_approach_b_distance_curve.py, 04_approach_c_thresholded_flags.py, 05_approach_a_toy_argmin.py, 07_approach_d_flag_sum.py — four of the five encrypted approaches, all on TenSEAL/SEAL. 06_cross_validate.py — diffs B and C against the plaintext reference. 03_fit_sign_polynomial.py — the offline comparator-polynomial fit consumed by A, C, D, and E. 08_sign_iteration_depth_wall.py — the encrypted self-composition depth-wall check for D.

09_approach_e_openfhe_bootstrap.py — Approach E, on OpenFHE instead of TenSEAL. This one needs a from-source OpenFHE + openfhe-python build (the pip wheel does not run on Darwin/arm64); it is not runnable with the same pip install as the other eight scripts. It imports its own openfhe module rather than the shared crypto_common.py the TenSEAL-based scripts use.

Each encrypted stage takes several minutes and holds a ~1.3–1.5 GB CKKS context in memory. Run them one at a time — running two at once (as we did once, by mistake, while iterating) roughly doubles both wall-clock time and peak memory for no benefit, and can trigger OS-level memory pressure on machines without headroom to spare.

Memory: a real, reportable cost

Peak RSS across the micro-benchmark's single process, which builds three separate CKKS contexts sequentially without disposing of the earlier ones, reached 9.65 GB. A single fresh encrypted OEM vector serializes to 6.86 MB; a single distance_sq ciphertext to 6.06 MB. None of this is exotic — it is the direct, load-bearing cost of CKKS's large polynomial ring degree (32768) needed to support 16 levels of depth — but it means a service holding several contexts or many live ciphertexts concurrently needs to actually budget for gigabytes, not megabytes, per concurrent unit of work. We hit this directly during development: running two heavy CKKS processes side by side while iterating on Approaches A and C exhausted available RAM on this machine and caused the OS to suspend the offending process. The fix was operational, not architectural — run one heavy CKKS process at a time — and every number in this report was produced that way.

Findings

Bad and good, without rounding up

What doesn't hold up

Approach A hits a real depth wall at exactly the point you'd want it not to. A single pairwise minimum (K=2) works and matches plaintext to 0.08%. A full 2-level reduction tree (K=4) throws ValueError: scale out of bounds partway through the second level. This is the genuine, reproducible ceiling of this context — not a toy limitation, since any useful screening resolution needs far more than 4 candidate points reduced to one.
A badly scaled comparator polynomial fails silently, not loudly — and that's the more dangerous failure mode. Before the scale constant was corrected (public dynamic range of distance² in this scenario spans ~30 to ~4.89 million km², and an initial guess of 2,000 was far too small), Approach C flagged 61 of 61 points as "below threshold" instead of the correct 1, and Approach A variously threw an exception or decrypted to physically nonsensical values (as large as 10⁹¹) that did not raise any error. A `decrypt()` call that doesn't throw is not evidence the result is right — only comparing against a plaintext ground truth caught this.
Thresholded disclosure (C) crushes resolution near the decision boundary by design. The scale needed to keep far-away points inside the comparator polynomial's valid domain (chosen from the observed ~4.89M km² max) means the flag near the actual threshold carries very low confidence: raw flag magnitude is ~0.00005–0.015 within 20 s of the true crossing, versus ~0.37–1.0 far from it. The binary decision is still correct here, but a customer who wanted the flag's margin would find it nearly unusable near the boundary.
Per-point cost is steep and doesn't shrink with cleverness. 7.6–10 seconds of wall-clock per grid point, per object pair, on this context. A screening service checking many object pairs at fine time resolution needs to budget CPU-seconds per candidate point, not per pair.
Zero-disclosure flag-sum (D) is real but not trustworthy here. Summing all 61 threshold flags before decrypting is a genuinely correct zero-per-point-disclosure design, and the sum itself is computed correctly (verified against manual plaintext summation). But interpreting that sum as a count fails badly (12 vs. the true 1) because the fitted sign polynomial only saturates near the window's edges. Sharpening it by self-composition works in plaintext but hits the identical depth wall as Approach A after only 2 extra compositions, when roughly 10 are needed. This backend cannot deliver a trustworthy zero-disclosure count at this precision.
Making bootstrapping work here required a from-source build and non-production security. TenSEAL (wrapping Microsoft SEAL) has never implemented CKKS bootstrapping — confirmed by introspecting the library directly, not assumed — and OpenFHE's own pip wheel (openfhe 1.5.1.0.24.4) is mislabeled as universal while actually bundling x86_64-linux-gnu shared objects, so it doesn't run on this Darwin/arm64 machine either. Approach E's result depends on a source build most deployments won't want to maintain, and on HEStd_NotSet security parameters at a ring dimension far below what a real deployment needs — untested at production scale, and plausibly one to two orders of magnitude slower per bootstrap there.
Approach E's composition count is tuned to this scenario, not proven general. 11 self-compositions were sized to this scenario's hardest point (5.432 km against a 10 km threshold); the script doesn't assert saturation was actually reached, it just reports the resulting sum. A candidate sitting closer to the threshold in a different scenario could need more compositions and bootstraps to produce an equally trustworthy count.

What holds up

The interpolation weights being public is a real, structural privacy improvement. Unlike the SGP4 study's polynomial-fit trick, none of these three approaches ever requires either party's orbit shape to be disclosed — only sample times, which are metadata, not trajectory content.
Approaches B and C are both correct, and both practical. B matches plaintext to within ~2 cm at the actual conjunction and ~7 m (3.2×10⁻⁶ relative) at the window's far edges; C's threshold decision agrees with plaintext on all 61 tested points. Neither depends on a deep reduction tree, so neither is exposed to the depth wall that breaks A.
The depth wall for A is now honestly characterized, not assumed. An earlier diagnosis mistook a scaling bug for "the depth wall failing silently" — a real methodological trap. Once the scale was corrected, the true, narrower finding is: K=2 succeeds cleanly, K=4 fails loudly and reproducibly at level 2. That is a precise, falsifiable claim about this context's limits, not a vague one.
The disclosure/cost tradeoff is legible and lets a customer actually choose. Full curve, thresholded flag, or full argmin map cleanly onto "how much do you trust the evaluating party," and the cost of each is now measured, not estimated — a real basis for a design conversation with a customer rather than a single fixed answer.
The flag-sum mechanism (ciphertext addition being free) is a real, reusable building block. Aggregating many per-point results into one decrypted number costs nothing extra in multiplicative depth. That primitive is sound; what's currently missing is a comparator sharp enough to feed it at this problem's precision, which is a depth-budget problem, not a design flaw in the aggregation itself.
Negative results were chased down to a root cause, not left as "it didn't work." The flag-sum's wrong count, and the failure of self-composition to fix it, both trace to the same measured, falsifiable limit: this backend supports at most 2 sequential polyval compositions after Approach C's own distance-and-flag pipeline before scale out of bounds, while saturating this problem's hardest point needs roughly 10 — a ~5× gap in sequential nonlinear evaluations, not a vague "FHE is too slow."
Bootstrapping genuinely fixes the exact problem that broke Approach D — independently verified, not taken on faith. On a from-source OpenFHE build, the same flag-sum pipeline with EvalBootstrap inserted between compositions ran all 11 needed compositions and produced a decrypted sum of 58.99999988 against an ideal 59, implying a count of 1 — matching the true ground truth and the same point (t=0) Approach C flags independently. We re-ran the full script from scratch and got an exact match, and separately reproduced a within-block corruption bug the implementation caught (56.4375 without masking down to one slot per block before summing, 59.0000 with it) by decrypting the raw ciphertext block before and after composition and reverting the fix by hand. This answers the question Approach D's negative result left open: the saturation ceiling is a property of TenSEAL/SEAL specifically, not of CKKS or FHE in general.
Glossary

Terms used above

OEM
Orbit Ephemeris Message — the CCSDS-standard table of time-stamped position/velocity samples describing a satellite's trajectory. Wikipedia (CCSDS)
CDM
Conjunction Data Message — the standard report of a predicted close approach between two objects: time, miss distance, and collision probability.
CPA
Closest Point of Approach — the time and distance at which two trajectories come nearest each other.
TLE
Two-Line Element set — the standard public format for an object's orbital elements. Wikipedia
CKKS
Cheon-Kim-Kim-Song — the approximate-arithmetic FHE scheme used throughout this study. Wikipedia
Lagrange interpolation
A method for estimating a value between known samples by constructing the unique polynomial passing through the nearest points.
Argmin
The input (here, a time) that minimizes a function — as opposed to the minimum value itself.
Horner's method
A way of evaluating a polynomial with the fewest possible multiplications, by nesting it.
k-d tree
A spatial data structure enabling fast nearest-neighbor search, used here to find the closest satellite pair among ~10,726 candidates.
Multiplicative depth
The fixed number of sequential ciphertext-ciphertext multiplications a CKKS context supports before it must be re-encrypted under a larger, more expensive context.
